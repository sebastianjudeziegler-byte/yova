Before 1914, Europe’s major powers were divided into two rival alliance blocs. These alliances were intended to increase security, but they also made international crises more dangerous because a dispute involving one country could draw in its partners.

## The two blocs

The **Triple Alliance** joined:

- Germany
- Austria-Hungary
- Italy

Opposing it was the **Triple Entente**, linking:

- France
- Russia
- Britain

“Entente” meant an understanding rather than one single, automatic military alliance. Even so, its members increasingly cooperated because they feared the power and intentions of the opposing bloc.

## Why division increased tension

Several tensions reinforced this split. France distrusted Germany, Russia competed with Austria-Hungary for influence in the Balkans, and Britain became concerned about Germany’s growing power. Alliances connected these separate rivalries.

A simple cause map is:

**existing rivalries → alliance blocs → fear of the opposing bloc → greater risk that a local crisis would spread**

For example, tension between Austria-Hungary and Russia was not isolated. Austria-Hungary could expect German support, while Russia was connected to France and Britain. This turned confrontation in one region into a potential European conflict.

A common mix-up is to treat the alliances as the single cause of World War I. They did **not** make war inevitable or cause fighting by themselves. Instead, they divided Europe before 1914 and created a system through which an immediate crisis could expand rapidly. Italy’s membership in the Triple Alliance also did not mean it automatically fought beside Germany and Austria-Hungary when war began."
