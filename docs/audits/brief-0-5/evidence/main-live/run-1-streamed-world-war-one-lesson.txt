## The big picture

Before 1914, Europe was divided into two rival alliance groups:

- **Triple Alliance:** Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy
- **Triple Entente:** Britain, France, and Russia

These alliances were intended to improve security. A country with powerful partners might discourage an enemy from attacking. However, alliances also created fear. Each group worried that the other was becoming stronger or preparing for war. As governments responded by strengthening their own alliances and armed forces, their rivals felt even more threatened.

This produced a cycle:

$$\\text{fear} \\rightarrow \\text{military preparation} \\rightarrow \\text{greater rival fear}$$

## Sources of tension

Several pressures made this divided Europe especially unstable:

- **Militarism:** European powers expanded their armies and navies and developed plans for rapid mobilisation.
- **Nationalism:** People strongly supported their nations, while some ethnic groups within large empires wanted independence.
- **Imperial rivalry:** Powerful countries competed for overseas territory, influence, and status.
- **Past disputes:** France resented Germany’s control of Alsace-Lorraine, while Austria-Hungary and Russia competed for influence in the Balkans.

These tensions connected. For example, Germany’s growing navy worried Britain. Britain then cooperated more closely with France and Russia. Germany could interpret that cooperation as an attempt to surround it, reinforcing its reliance on Austria-Hungary. An action intended to increase one country’s safety therefore increased another country’s fear.

## Why alliances mattered

A local dispute now had the potential to involve several powers. If Austria-Hungary entered a conflict, its connection with Germany mattered. Russia’s support for Serbia could then bring Russia into opposition with them, while Russia’s partnerships linked the crisis to France and Britain.

A common mix-up is to treat alliances as if they automatically caused war. They did not make war inevitable, and not every commitment was identical. Italy, for example, did not immediately fight alongside Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1914. The crucial point is that rival commitments and expectations made leaders fear abandoning allies and allowed a regional crisis to expand.

Europe was therefore tense before 1914 because alliances, military preparation, nationalism, imperial competition, and unresolved rivalries divided the continent into suspicious camps."
