Photosynthesis: limiting factors — GCSE biology revision notes

Photosynthesis transfers energy from light into chemical energy stored in glucose. Carbon dioxide and water are reactants; glucose and oxygen are products. Chlorophyll absorbs light in chloroplasts.

Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature can each limit the rate of photosynthesis. Increasing one factor raises the rate only while that factor is limiting. A plateau does not mean the plant has stopped photosynthesising: it means another factor may now limit the rate.

Temperature affects enzymes. Increasing temperature raises the rate up to an optimum; temperatures beyond the optimum can reduce the rate as enzymes denature. This is different from the usual rising-then-plateau response to increasing light intensity while temperature remains controlled.

In a pondweed experiment, oxygen production can estimate photosynthesis rate. Collecting oxygen volume over a fixed time is more reliable than counting bubbles because bubbles can vary in size. Keep pondweed length, measurement time and other conditions constant when changing light intensity. A lamp can heat the water, so controlling water temperature matters.

Example: doubling lamp brightness does not increase oxygen production, but adding carbon dioxide does. At that point carbon dioxide was limiting; light intensity was not. More light alone would not solve that limitation.

Practice goal: interpret experimental evidence and explain the limiting factor. Do not merely memorise the definitions.
