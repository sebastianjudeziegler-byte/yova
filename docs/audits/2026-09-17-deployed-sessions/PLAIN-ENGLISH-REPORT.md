# What the deployed YOVA sessions are actually like

Audit date: 17 September 2026. Tested the [live app](https://www.yovaapp.com), verified against deployed commit `b75ec8d`. Brief 2 and unmerged work are excluded.

During the write-up, a newer plan-Undo fix (`eeb1ff9`) deployed. I checked the full change list: it did not change the session code reviewed here or implement the Brief 2 plan rewrite.

**My verdict: YOVA can provide a useful short guided exercise, but I would not yet rely on this version for substantial exam practice.** The strongest part is identifying an initial mistake. The weakest parts are providing enough worthwhile work and checking that the mistake has actually been repaired. Your concerns about short active-recall sessions and concept mapping were supported by these tests, although some parts worked better than that description might suggest.

The long biology journey made the problem particularly clear. A learner asking for A-level application practice received a short explanation, five multiple-choice questions and, after one deliberately wrong answer, one retry question. The session reached its completion screen at 5:23 on a 55-minute timer. That is an automated audit time, including inspection and generation waits, not a measurement of how long a student would take. Nevertheless, allocating 20 minutes to reading, 25 to these questions and 10 to review gave a poor account of the actual work available. Even the one-question retry retained the 25-minute allocation.

The questions did include application, comparison and misconception checks. They were not all definition recall. But several wrong choices were easy to dismiss, and the learner never had to produce an answer without seeing options, interpret numerical results or work through a demanding unfamiliar example. The retry correctly targeted the missed point, but mainly changed dye spreading through water into smell spreading through air. That is useful basic reinforcement; it is limited evidence of being able to apply the idea independently. Finishing quickly should be allowed. The improvement is worthwhile optional work and honest expectations, not forcing someone to spend 40 minutes repeating easy material.

Concept mapping was a weak interaction. I entered concepts in text boxes and then separately typed a starting concept, relationship and destination. No map appeared. It felt more like completing a form about relationships than building something I could inspect and reorganize visually. However, the feedback was useful: when I deliberately reversed the direction of osmosis, it identified the exact incorrect relationship and explained the problem. The next step undermined that success. I entered the same misconception again as my repair, and the session immediately moved to completion without checking it. It did not say the repair was correct, but it provided no way to find out whether the intended correction had happened.

The mathematics journey showed a similar pattern. Trying before studying worked: I could submit an explanation before seeing the teaching. Feedback correctly caught the mistake of multiplying two derivatives instead of using the product rule. My revised worked answer then went unchecked, while the ending still displayed the original missing/incorrect counts. A clearer ending would distinguish the original feedback from the revised answer and offer a targeted recheck.

**Personalization is real, but some explanations of it are untrue.** Profile changes affected method, timer, example support and, in mathematics, step order. However, biology claimed I had answered before studying when it actually started with teaching. Mathematics later claimed practice had asked an extra comparison question when no such question occurred. One tip also diagnosed an error in term order that did not match my submitted mistake. The app adjusts delivery more convincingly than it demonstrates adaptation to the learner's ability. It needs to describe what actually happened rather than turn profile answers into unsupported claims about the learner.

There were also concrete reliability failures. Leaving the concept-map session and resuming returned me to the right step but erased all six unsubmitted field values. Finishing both biology sessions produced save errors; the first failed again on retry. Later, their plans still showed 0/1 complete. Mathematics saved successfully and showed 1/1 complete. This establishes a real problem in those attempts, not a general failure rate or a diagnosis of the cause.

The outside-study journey worked through its learning steps. I uploaded a small set of revision notes; YOVA gave relevant directions, then asked questions grounded in those notes when I returned. Its five-question mix included more factual recall than the detail-oriented profile's round, so that preference had a visible effect. Both the 15-minute and 55-minute journeys nevertheless began with five questions. In the outside-study round, all five correct answers appeared first. The other round had varied positions, so this is one concerning example, not a measured pattern across the app. Pause, extra time and hide/show controls worked. This fourth session also failed to save, including on retry: three different sessions had save failures, while mathematics succeeded.

Brief 2 is important context. Topic selection, coverage, progression and content-based time estimates belong partly to that upcoming work. The mathematics request becoming a foundational explanation session, for example, cannot fairly be blamed entirely on the new session system. These tests do not establish that the implementation order was wrong. But a new plan generator will not automatically preserve drafts, fix completion, make maps visual, verify repairs or correct inaccurate personalization messages.

My priorities are to make finish/resume dependable; provide practice suited to the actual learning target; check chosen repairs; make mapping usable; and make session claims accurate. The technical handoff separates reproduced failures from additional issues found only in source code.

The tests used an existing test account, with contrasting profiles on that same account and four new Study Now sessions. They were not separate new users or a controlled comparison with identical generated topics. They also do not measure retention or establish the value of complete multi-session plans. The original profile answers were restored; audit-created plans and notes remain available. Within those limits, the session weaknesses are substantial enough to address before presenting YOVA as a dependable main study tool.
